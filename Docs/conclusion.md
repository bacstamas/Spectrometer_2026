# Conclusion
The primary objective of this thesis was the design, construction, and validation of a low-cost absorption spectrophotometer intended for educational use. The developed instrument combines simple hardware with dedicated software for data acquisition, visualization, and absorbance calculation. Experimental testing demonstrated that the spectrometer is capable of producing reproducible measurements and calibration curves that follow the linear behavior predicted by the Beer-Lambert law. While the measurements exhibit a non-negligible level of noise, the results show that the instrument is sufficiently reliable for educational and demonstration purposes, provided that appropriate care is taken during sample preparation and measurement.

One of the main strengths of the instrument is its combination of low cost and transparency. Unlike commercial spectrophotometers, whose internal operation is often hidden from the user, the present design exposes the essential elements of an absorption measurement system and makes the underlying physical principles easier to understand. As a result, the spectrometer is well suited for use in secondary-school and undergraduate laboratory activities. Students can gain practical experience with solution preparation, reference measurements, absorbance calculations, the construction of calibration curves, and the quantitative determination of concentrations. More advanced educational projects could even include the determination of the molar absorptivity of a substance through Beer-Lambert analysis.

Beyond its use as a measurement instrument, the reproduction of the spectrometer itself could serve as a valuable educational project for physics students at the BSc or MSc level. Constructing the device requires engagement with several important areas of modern scientific and engineering practice, including absorption spectroscopy, 3D design and printing, microcontroller programming, object-oriented software development, and experimental testing. The process of building and validating the instrument provides a deeper understanding of both the physical principles and the practical challenges involved in spectroscopic measurements.

Although the instrument successfully fulfills its intended educational purpose, several opportunities for further development remain. One possible improvement would be the replacement of the AS7262 sensor with a sensor offering a larger number of spectral channels, increasing the spectral resolution of the measurements. Another possibility would be to adopt a more traditional spectrometer architecture based on a rotatable diffraction grating and a simpler photodetector, allowing the acquisition of a continuous spectrum rather than measurements at six discrete wavelengths. Such modifications could improve the analytical capabilities of the instrument while preserving its educational value.

A particularly promising direction for future development would be the replacement of the Arduino Uno with an ESP32 microcontroller. The additional processing power and built-in Wi-Fi connectivity of the ESP32 would make it possible to implement the user interface as a web application hosted directly by the instrument. In such a design, users could access the spectrometer through any device equipped with a web browser, without the need to install dedicated software. This would simplify deployment in classroom environments, reduce platform-dependence, and further increase the accessibility of the system.

In conclusion, the developed spectrophotometer demonstrates that quantitative absorption measurements can be achieved with inexpensive and widely available components. Although it cannot compete with commercial instruments in terms of precision or spectral resolution, it successfully fulfills its intended role as an educational tool and provides a practical platform for teaching both spectroscopy and scientific instrumentation.
